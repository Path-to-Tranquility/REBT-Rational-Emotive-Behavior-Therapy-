REBT / THOUGHT RECORD

Talk through your thoughts.

Fill in your REBT record by speaking, then review and save your answers. Use the manual form.

Select Start guided voice form. Each question is read aloud, then the microphone listens to your English answer. Pause for about a second and a half to finish, or select Done answering. Your answer appears on screen: say yes to keep it in the form, or redo to answer again. At the end, the full record is read back and you are asked for permission to save to CSV.

You can interrupt the confirmation prompt: say yes, correct, or yup to keep your answer; say no, na, not at all, or redo to answer again. Wait until the initial question ends before giving your answer. Say ?skip? for the optional practice plan. You can use the buttons to confirm or redo. Stop voice to edit by typing; an unconfirmed answer is discarded.

Speech recognition depends on your browser and may require internet access. Your browser may send audio to its speech service for processing. This app saves only the reviewed text to your local CSV after your final save confirmation.

Choose one specific situation and work through A–E in order. A few sentences in your own words are enough. The examples follow one situation to show what belongs in each section; your answers may be different. All fields are required except your next action.

Describe one moment: where you were, who was involved, and what was said or done. Write what a camera could record. Put your interpretation of what it means in B.

Example: “I ordered a pair of shoes online. The delivery date was yesterday, but the parcel has not arrived. The tracking page now says it is delayed.”

Write the thoughts you had about the event, even if they seem harsh or unreasonable now. What did it mean about you, other people, or the future? Look for demands such as “I must,” “they should,” “I cannot stand it,” or judgments about your whole worth.

Example: “My order must arrive exactly when promised. This delay is completely unbearable. The seller does not care about me, and I cannot stand waiting.”

C — Consequences: how did you respond?

Describe your response to the event and the beliefs in B. Separate what you felt from what you did.

Name the unpleasant emotions you actually felt, such as anxiety, shame, anger, guilt, or sadness. “I felt like a failure” is a belief; “I felt ashamed” names an emotion. You can also note physical sensations.

Example: “I felt furious and anxious. My jaw tightened and my heart beat faster.”

Describe what you actually did next, or avoided doing. Include actions such as withdrawing, arguing, checking repeatedly, or putting something off. If you only had an urge to act, say that.

Example: “I refreshed the tracking page every few minutes, sent an angry message to the seller, and put off my other plans while waiting.”

Choose one strong belief from B. Ask: What evidence supports it, and what evidence challenges it? Does wanting something mean it must happen? Does one mistake define my whole worth? Does holding this belief help me respond usefully? Write your answers, not just the questions.

Example: “The delivery is late, but that alone does not prove the seller does not care about me. Wanting it on time does not mean delays cannot happen. Waiting is inconvenient, but I have handled delays before. Repeatedly checking the tracking page does not make it arrive sooner.”

E — Effective new belief and response

Write a more flexible belief, then describe the emotion and behaviour you want to work toward. These can be intentions even if your feelings have not changed yet.

Replace the rigid demand in B with a preference. Acknowledge the difficulty, what you can tolerate, and your ability to choose a response. Make it believable to you.

Example: “I strongly prefer deliveries to arrive on time. This delay is frustrating, but I can tolerate the wait and ask for a clear update. I can take practical action and still get on with my day.”

Name a realistic unpleasant emotion that would let you acknowledge the problem and still respond helpfully—for example, concern, disappointment, sadness, or regret. You do not need to force yourself to feel happy. Describe the emotion you would like to work toward.

Example: “Disappointment about the late delivery and concern about when it will arrive, while still feeling able to handle the situation.”

Describe a specific, helpful action you can choose in this situation. Focus on something within your control. Connect it to the behaviour in C that you want to change.

Example: “I will send a polite message asking for an updated delivery date, choose one time to check tracking again, and continue with my other plans.”

Make the constructive behaviour easier to start: when and where will you take the first step?

Example: “At 3 p.m. today, I will spend five minutes contacting customer support with my order number. I will check tracking again tomorrow morning.”

Rate your overall emotional distress about this situation before this reflection and now: 0 = none, 10 = strongest. Record how you actually feel, even if the rating has stayed the same or increased.
